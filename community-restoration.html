Species Planted

Phormium cookianum

Mountain Flax Slope stabilisation & bird habitat

A robust flax species well suited to South Auckland's conditions. Forms large clumps that provide shelter for small birds and help stabilise soil on slopes.

Carex secta

Pūrei Riparian restoration & water filtering

A native sedge that thrives in wet conditions near waterways. It supports riparian restoration by filtering sediment from runoff before it enters streams.

Carex virgata

Pūrei Wetland margin & invertebrate support

Another native sedge species ideal for stream and wetland margins. Together with Carex secta, it creates a diverse ground layer that supports invertebrate communities.

Apodasmia similis

Oioi Soil erosion prevention & pollutant filter

An endemic New Zealand reed normally located in coastal areas, wetlands, and estuaries, making it a cornerstone plant for environmental restoration.

Interview with Shane McNeil

Community Ranger, South Auckland, Sustainable Papakura

Q: Why was this particular site chosen for restoration work?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger This site serves as an important stormwater runoff area. During periods of heavy rainfall, water from the surrounding roads flows into the reserve. There is also a stormwater outlet located on the site, which channels excess water into the nearby stream. The native plants we are currently planting help stabilize the soil, reducing erosion and sediment runoff. They also act as natural filters, absorbing toxins and heavy metals from the water before they can enter the stream, improving overall water quality.

Q: What was this area like before restoration efforts?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger Not much, really. The area was mostly covered in mown grass, with very little native vegetation present.

Q: What are the biggest ongoing environmental issues that are affecting this area?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger Basically, just the presence of harmful toxins and heavy metals in the water. Also, increased urbanisation has also contributed to the problem.

Q: How has human caused urban development impacted this area?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger More pollutants are washed from roads and urban areas into the stormwater system and nearby stream. It has also reduced and fragmented available planting space, leaving only small, isolated patches of vegetation that are less effective at supporting biodiversity.

Q: So, what species are we planting today?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger We are currently planting a variety native grasses including flax which is phormium cookianum, carex secta and carex virgata.

Q: How do you decide what species to plant?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger These species are well adapted to wet environments and have strong root systems, so they do not get uprooted or washed away during heavy rainfall events.

Q: Does planting improve biodiversity in this area?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger Yes, certainly! Planting native species helps improve biodiversity by creating habitat and shelter for local wildlife. These plants provide cover for bird species and can also support insects, contributing to a healthier ecosystem.

Q: What are the long-term goals for this restoration project?

Shane McNeil — Community Ranger We would love to carry out water quality testing in the future to measure how much improvement the planting has made over time. Another important goal is to see native biodiversity gradually return to the area.
